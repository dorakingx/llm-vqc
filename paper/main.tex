\title{Semantic Priors, Not Feedback Loops:\\

\author{Tomoya Hatanaka}

\begin{abstract}
Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used as agents that design variational
quantum circuits, but it is rarely tested which part of such an agent helps.
We study quantum-autoencoder architecture search for spin-chain ground states under
exact capacity control: every candidate from every method has $3n$ trainable rotations
and $n$ CNOTs on $n$ qubits, and all methods share the optimizer, data, evaluation
budget and validation-only selection. We prove that the best achievable trash fidelity
equals a Ky Fan eigenvalue sum, which is $1-O(10^{-7})$ for every task here, so all
shortfalls measure constrained search, and that real (RY, CNOT) encoders lose nothing
for real ground states.
A one-factor robustness study (budget, width $n\in\{4,6,8\}$, TFIM versus XXZ, two
model snapshots) initially suggests that a closed feedback loop helps at small budgets
and hurts at large widths. We show that this pattern is an artifact: the open-loop arm
reuses one generated pool on all 12 seeds and selects the same circuit in all seeds in
five of seven conditions. Using only logged LLM outputs, we build arms that replicate
generation per seed. Score-free LLM proposals beat budget-matched random search in all
seven conditions ($+0.10$ to $+0.19$ in trash fidelity, Holm $p\le0.024$), whereas
incumbent-conditioned redesign shows no detectable benefit over fresh score-free
proposals that share the same warm start in any condition (all 95\% CIs cover zero).
Prior-matched non-LLM baselines locate the remaining value: sampling with only the
prompt's two structural facts (RY rotations, nearest-neighbour CNOTs) recovers
36--88\% of the LLM's advantage over uniform search, two hand-written ladder templates
recover 80--93\% on the TFIM tasks and exceed the open-loop LLM on XXZ, and
permuting the LLM's gate order removes most of its edge at six and eight qubits.
On this task, the LLM is useful as a fast translator of stated structure into a
proposal distribution, not as an iterative optimizer.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Variational quantum algorithms rely on parameterized circuits whose
\emph{architecture}---which gates act on which qubits, and in what
order---fixes expressivity, trainability and hardware cost
\cite{bharti2022nisq,sim2019,mcclean2018barren}.
Architecture choice is discrete and every candidate must be trained before it
can be scored, so quantum architecture search (QAS) operates with small
evaluation budgets \cite{du2022,zhang2022dqas,kuo2021qasrl,ostaszewski2021structure,grimsley2019adapt}.
Large language models (LLMs) are an attractive proposer in this regime: they can
read a physical task description and translate it into structural hypotheses
before any candidate is trained.
A fast-growing literature uses LLM agents to design ans\"atze, feature maps and
state-preparation circuits
\cite{liang2023qgas,knipfer2026,sakka2025featuremap,sakka2026,cao2025funsearch,finger2026hive,gujju2025qcbm,ueda2025qgan,macarone2026testtime,bhatia2026qrc}.
Most of these studies are system demonstrations: the agent is compared with
hand-designed circuits or with the literature, rarely with a non-semantic search
under the same circuit capacity and evaluation budget, and rarely across more than
one generation of the LLM output.
In classical neural architecture search, the analogous omission was costly:
budget-matched random search turned out to be a strong baseline that many
sophisticated methods did not beat \cite{li2019randomnas,bergstra2012random}.

This paper is an evaluation study.
We ask \emph{when} LLM proposals improve variational-circuit architecture search,
and \emph{which} part of an LLM agent is responsible: the score-free prior encoded
in its first proposals, or the closed feedback loop that refines them.
We use a quantum-autoencoder (QAE) task \cite{romero2017}---compress spin-chain
ground states into half of the register---because its objective is a direct quantum
quantity whose information-theoretic ceiling can be computed exactly, and because
its circuit budget can be fixed exactly.
Every candidate from every method is an ordered list of $3n$ trainable rotations
and $n$ CNOTs on $n$ qubits; the optimizer, data, budget and selection rule are
shared; the LLM never sets an angle.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{A capacity-controlled benchmark with an exact ceiling.}
We show (Prop.~\ref{prop:ceiling}) that the best achievable trash fidelity of any
encoder equals a Ky Fan eigenvalue sum of the ensemble state, which is
$1-O(10^{-7})$ for every task studied; all observed shortfalls therefore measure
constrained search and training, not the compressibility of the data.
We also prove that for real ground states nothing is lost by restricting to real
(RY, CNOT) encoders (Prop.~\ref{prop:real}).
\item \textbf{A one-factor robustness study} (budget, width, Hamiltonian family,
model) of four budget-matched arms---Random, Greedy, an open-loop LLM batch and a
closed-loop LLM with free-form redesign---with protocols frozen in Git before
each run.
\item \textbf{A replication-unit analysis.} The open-loop LLM arm selects the same
single architecture in all 12 seeds in five of seven conditions, so its seeds
replicate training, not generation. Using only already-logged LLM outputs, we build
per-seed-replicated comparisons that separate the semantic prior from feedback
(Secs.~\ref{sec:warm}--\ref{sec:matched}).
\item \textbf{Prior-matched non-LLM baselines.} We test whether the LLM's advantage
is reproduced by hand-coding the facts the prompt states (real amplitudes
$\Rightarrow$ RY; nearest-neighbour chain $\Rightarrow$ nearest-neighbour CNOTs) and
quantify the LLM prior as an equivalent number of uniform-random evaluations
(Prop.~\ref{prop:budget}, Sec.~\ref{sec:prior}).
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Summary of findings.}
(i) The score-free semantic prior is robust: when LLM generation is replicated per
seed it beats budget-matched random search in all seven conditions.
(ii) The closed feedback loop has no detectable value once its open-loop comparator
shares its warm start and is replicated per seed; the closed-loop advantages and
reversals of a conventional analysis came from reusing one open-loop pool.
(iii) A sampler that encodes only the prompt's two structural facts recovers
36--88\% of the LLM's advantage over uniform search, two hand-written templates recover
80--93\% on TFIM, and much of the rest is gate ordering.


\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{LLMs as circuit designers.}
QGAS used GPT-4 to propose ans\"atze for chemistry and finance tasks and reported
results comparable to QAS methods \cite{liang2023qgas}.
Agentic systems now design quantum feature maps \cite{sakka2025featuremap}, VQE
ans\"atze and feature maps with web-retrieved knowledge \cite{sakka2026}, Born-machine
\cite{gujju2025qcbm} and QGAN \cite{ueda2025qgan} ans\"atze, and general VQCs from
training feedback \cite{knipfer2026}.
Program-search approaches pair an LLM with an evolutionary loop:
FunSearch-style discovery of scalable state-preparation circuits for spin chains
\cite{cao2025funsearch,romeraparedes2024funsearch} and the Hive platform for molecular
ground-state heuristics run on hardware \cite{finger2026hive}.
These works establish that LLMs \emph{can} produce useful circuits; their baselines
are hand-designed circuits or prior methods.
Two recent studies are closest to ours in using matched budgets.
Macarone-Palmieri and Lo Franco compare a memory-augmented, restart-from-best LLM
loop with budget-matched random hill climbing for entanglement maximization
\cite{macarone2026testtime}; Bhatia and Naik compare random, evolutionary and TPE
search with an LLM agent and an LLM--evolution hybrid at 25 evaluations for quantum
reservoir design, and find the LLM most useful inside a classical search loop
\cite{bhatia2026qrc}.
Our study differs in three ways: candidate \emph{capacity} is fixed exactly, so no
arm can win by proposing larger circuits; we separate the score-free prior from the
feedback loop with arms that share their first half; and we test hand-coded priors
derived from the same prompt facts.

\paragraph{LLMs as optimizers and the random-search baseline.}
LLMs have been used as black-box optimizers that read scored history
\cite{yang2024opro}, as components of Bayesian optimization \cite{liu2024llambo}, and
as mutation operators in neural architecture search \cite{chen2023evoprompting}.
In NAS, budget-matched random search is a strong and often unbeaten baseline
\cite{li2019randomnas,bergstra2012random}; outside quantum computing, agentic research
loops have recently been compared directly with random search at fixed budgets
\cite{li2026autoresearch}. Benchmarks that evaluate QAS beyond a single scalar are
emerging \cite{niu2026hamqasbench}.

\paragraph{Quantum architecture search and quantum autoencoders.}
QAS methods include supernet-based search \cite{du2022}, differentiable relaxation
\cite{zhang2022dqas}, reinforcement learning \cite{kuo2021qasrl}, structure
optimization \cite{ostaszewski2021structure} and adaptive operator growth
\cite{grimsley2019adapt}; embedding-aware QAS couples data encoding and circuit
structure \cite{dai2026}.
QAEs compress families of states into a latent register by disentangling a trash
register \cite{romero2017}; they have been applied to anomaly detection
\cite{frehner2025,elalami2025}, and QAE architectures have been searched with genetic
and NAS methods \cite{agha2025,kulshrestha2025}. We are not aware of prior work that
evaluates LLM-proposed QAE architectures against capacity- and budget-matched search.

\section{Task and theoretical reference points}
\label{sec:task}

\subsection{Quantum-autoencoder task}

For an even number of qubits $n$ we use two real, nearest-neighbour Hamiltonian
families on an open chain,
\begin{align}
H_{\rm TFIM}(h) &= -\sum_{i=0}^{n-2} Z_iZ_{i+1} - h\sum_{i=0}^{n-1} X_i,\\
H_{\rm XXZ}(\Delta) &= \sum_{i=0}^{n-2}\bigl(X_iX_{i+1}+Y_iY_{i+1}+\Delta Z_iZ_{i+1}\bigr),
\end{align}
with $h,\Delta\in[0.2,2.0]$, and take $\ket{\psi(\lambda)}$ to be the exact ground
state (phase-fixed so that its largest amplitude is real and positive).
Qubits $L=\{0,\dots,n/2-1\}$ form the latent register and $T=\{n/2,\dots,n-1\}$
the trash register.
An encoder $U$ is scored on a finite set $S$ of ground states by the
\emph{trash fidelity}
\begin{equation}
F_{\rm trash}(U;S)=\frac{1}{|S|}\sum_{\psi\in S}\bra{\psi}U^\dagger\Pi_T U\ket{\psi},
\qquad \Pi_T = \mathbb{I}_L\otimes\ket{0\cdots0}\!\bra{0\cdots0}_T ,
\label{eq:ftrash}
\end{equation}
the probability that all trash qubits are found in $\ket 0$.
$F_{\rm trash}=1$ means the trash register is exactly the reference state and all
input-dependent information has been routed into $L$, which is the compression
criterion of Romero \emph{et al.}~\cite{romero2017}; it is a compression-side
quantity and is not by itself an end-to-end reconstruction fidelity.
Each seed draws 32 training and 12 validation parameters uniformly; a fixed grid of
64 parameters ($0.215$ to $1.985$) is the held-out test set.
The test grid is the same for every seed and every condition; it is never used for
training, selection or feedback.

\subsection{The compression ceiling}

\begin{proposition}[Compression ceiling]
\label{prop:ceiling}
Let $\bar\rho_S=|S|^{-1}\sum_{\psi\in S}\ket\psi\!\bra\psi$ have eigenvalues
$\lambda_1\ge\lambda_2\ge\cdots$. For every unitary $U$,
\begin{equation}
F_{\rm trash}(U;S)\le F^\star(S) := \sum_{i=1}^{2^{|L|}}\lambda_i(\bar\rho_S),
\end{equation}
and the bound is attained.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
$F_{\rm trash}(U;S)=\operatorname{tr}(P\bar\rho_S)$ with $P=U^\dagger\Pi_TU$, an
orthogonal projector of rank $2^{|L|}$. By Ky Fan's maximum principle
\cite{fan1949theorem}, $\max_P \operatorname{tr}(P\bar\rho_S)$ over rank-$k$ projectors is
the sum of the $k$ largest eigenvalues. Equality holds for any $U$ that maps the
$2^{|L|}$ leading eigenvectors of $\bar\rho_S$ onto the computational basis states
whose trash bits are all zero.
\end{proof}

Table~\ref{tab:ceiling} evaluates $F^\star$ on the test grid.
The ceiling is $1-O(10^{-7})$ or closer in every condition; at $n=4$ the two leading
eigenvalues alone already carry $0.9994$ of the weight.
Consequently, every shortfall reported below---the best method reaches
$0.97$ at $n=4$ and $0.88$ at $n=8$---comes from the fixed circuit capacity
($3n$ rotations, $n$ CNOTs), the short training schedule, and the search, not from
the data being incompressible.
The benchmark therefore measures how well a proposer finds \emph{trainable}
circuits inside a small fixed budget.

\subsection{Real encoders suffice}

\begin{proposition}[Real encoders]
\label{prop:real}
If every $\ket\psi\in S$ has real amplitudes, the ceiling $F^\star(S)$ is attained
by a real orthogonal $U\in SO(2^n)$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
$\bar\rho_S$ is then real symmetric, so it has a real orthonormal eigenbasis
$\{v_i\}$. The matrix $U$ whose rows are the $v_i$, ordered so that the leading
$2^{|L|}$ map to trash-zero basis states, is real orthogonal and attains the bound by
Prop.~\ref{prop:ceiling}. If $\det U=-1$, flip the sign of one row; this changes no
fidelity.
\end{proof}

Both families are real in the computational basis, so their ground states can be
chosen real (the prompt states exactly this fact).
RY rotations and CNOTs are real orthogonal gates, whereas RX and RZ map real states to
complex ones.
Prop.~\ref{prop:real} therefore guarantees that an ``RY-only'' prior gives up nothing
at the ceiling; whether it helps at \emph{fixed} capacity is an empirical question
that Sec.~\ref{sec:prior} answers.

\subsection{A tail-mass model of budgeted search}

To reason about budgets we idealize a proposer as drawing candidates whose
(validation-selected) score exceeds a level $\tau$ with probability $q(\tau)$,
independently across draws.

\begin{proposition}[Equivalent budget]
\label{prop:budget}
Under independent draws, best-of-$B$ reaches $\tau$ with probability
$1-(1-q)^B$. A proposer with tail mass $q$ at budget $B$ is matched by a proposer with
tail mass $p<q$ only at budget
\begin{equation}
B_{\rm eq}=B\,\frac{\ln(1-q)}{\ln(1-p)}\;\approx\;B\,\frac{q}{p}\quad(p,q\ll1).
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proposition}[Open versus closed loop]
\label{prop:loop}
Let an open-loop arm make $B$ independent draws with tail mass $q$, and a closed-loop
arm make $B/2$ such draws followed by $B/2$ refinements, each of which reaches $\tau$
with probability $r$ given that no earlier candidate has. Then the closed loop is more
likely to reach $\tau$ if and only if $r>q$, for every even $B$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The failure probabilities are $(1-q)^B$ and $(1-q)^{B/2}(1-r)^{B/2}$.
\end{proof}

Proposition~\ref{prop:loop} is deliberately simple, but it makes a sharp
prediction: under independent draws the sign of the closed-minus-open difference does
not depend on the budget. A strongly budget-dependent sign, as observed in
Sec.~\ref{sec:robustness}, must therefore come from a violation of independence---for
example, an open-loop arm that reuses \emph{one} generated pool for every seed, so
that all seeds share the same draw---or from refinement quality that depends on the
incumbent. Sections~\ref{sec:replication} and \ref{sec:matched} test the first
explanation directly.

\section{Search protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

\subsection{Capacity-controlled neutral space}

At $n$ qubits every candidate is an ordered sequence of exactly $4n$ gates: $3n$
trainable single-qubit rotations, each with a free axis in $\{X,Y,Z\}$ and a free qubit,
and $n$ CNOTs, each with a free ordered (control, target) pair; repeated pairs are
allowed and the ordering is free.
At $n=4$ this is 12 rotations and 4 CNOTs.
Capacity is checked programmatically for every candidate of every method; a method
cannot win by proposing a larger circuit.

\paragraph{Shared trainer and selection.}
All $3n$ angles are initialized from $U(-0.05,0.05)$ with a seed derived from
(seed, architecture hash) and trained by Adam (learning rate $0.05$, 60 epochs,
complex128 state vectors, noiseless) on $1-F_{\rm trash}$ of the training states;
the checkpoint with the best validation loss is kept.
Each method evaluates exactly $B$ candidates per seed and reports the candidate with
the lowest validation loss; its held-out test $F_{\rm trash}$ is read once, after
selection. The LLM never chooses angles and never sees a test value.

\subsection{Arms}

\begin{description}[leftmargin=0pt,itemsep=2pt]
\item[Random.] $B$ independent uniform draws from the neutral space.
\item[Greedy.] $B/2$ uniform warm starts; the best by validation becomes the
incumbent; $B/2$ single structural mutations (axis, qubit, control, target, or a swap
of two positions), each accepted only if it improves validation.
\item[LLM-Open.] One call to a version-pinned model requests $B$ distinct candidates
from a task card that states the Hamiltonian, that its ground states can be chosen
real, that interactions are nearest-neighbour on the chain, the latent/trash split, and
the exact resource contract (Appendix~\ref{app:prompt}). No score is ever shown.
\emph{The pool is generated once per condition and evaluated on all 12 seeds.}
\item[LLM-Closed.] Per seed, one score-free call requests $B/2$ structurally diverse
candidates (the \emph{warm batch}); then $B/2$ sequential calls each receive the
incumbent architecture and its validation fidelity, may redesign it freely within
capacity, and return one new candidate, accepted only if validation improves.
\end{description}
Both LLM arms use temperature $0.7$, structured JSON output, at most two JSON-repair
retries and a bounded capacity repair; a proposal that still violates the contract,
or duplicates an evaluated candidate, is replaced by a \emph{flagged random draw}
that consumes budget and stays in the analysis, so LLM scores are operational scores
that pay for the model's own specification failures.
The reference model is \texttt{gpt-5.4-mini-2026-03-17}; the alternative is
\texttt{gpt-4.1-mini-2025-04-14}.

\subsection{Robustness design and protocol freezing}
\label{sec:design}

The reference cell is $n=4$, TFIM, $B=8$, reference model. Six further conditions each
change exactly one factor: $B\in\{4,16\}$; $n\in\{6,8\}$; XXZ instead of TFIM; the
alternative model. The width rule scales capacity identically for all methods, and a
manifest fingerprint of every frozen element (prompt template, schema, trainer, data
streams, selection rule, method logic, random streams and analysis conventions) is
checked to differ from the reference in exactly one factor.
Each study's protocol was committed to the public repository before its runs; the
commit order is independently checkable. This is protocol freezing in version control,
not registration with an external registry.

\paragraph{Development history and forking paths.}
The reference cell is the fifth design on this task (Appendix~\ref{app:history}): three
earlier closed-loop interfaces were tried, on the same test grid, before the one
studied here. We therefore treat the reference-cell closed-loop result as
\emph{hypothesis-generating} and base conclusions on the six conditions that were run
after the design was frozen and on the analyses of Secs.~\ref{sec:warm}--\ref{sec:prior}.

\subsection{Statistics}

All comparisons are paired by seed (12 seeds; identical data streams across arms).
We report the mean paired gain, a bootstrap 95\% CI (10{,}000 resamples), Cohen's
$d_z$, win counts, and exact two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank $p$-values.
Within each family of contrasts we also report Holm-adjusted $p$-values
\cite{holm1979simple}.
\textbf{Unit of replication.} Seeds replicate data sampling and initialization. They
replicate LLM \emph{generation} only for arms that call the model per seed
(LLM-Closed and the arms of Secs.~\ref{sec:warm}--\ref{sec:matched}); for LLM-Open all
seeds share one generated pool, so seed-level tests involving LLM-Open quantify
uncertainty about that particular pool, not about the model.

\section{Robustness study}
\label{sec:robustness}

Table~\ref{tab:robustness-means} gives the selected-architecture test fidelity of
the four arms in all seven conditions, and Table~\ref{tab:robustness-contrasts} the
two contrasts that the original analysis plan treated as primary.
Read at face value, the study says two things.
First, LLM-Open $-$ Random keeps its sign in all six varied conditions and is
significant in four; LLM-Closed $-$ Random is significant in all six; Greedy $-$ Random
is significant in none and reverses at $B=4$, so validation-guided local refinement
without semantics does not explain the gap.
Second, the closed-loop contrast moves monotonically against the closed loop along
both ladders---$+0.081\to+0.007\to-0.002$ as $B$ grows and $+0.007\to-0.049\to-0.075$ as
$n$ grows---and significantly reverses at six and eight qubits.
Contract compliance is a separate failure mode: the share of evaluated LLM candidates
that satisfied the exact resource contract falls from $95.8\%$ to $84.9\%$ at $n=6$ and
to $65.1\%$ with the alternative model, and every invalid proposal is replaced by a
flagged random draw inside the score.

By Prop.~\ref{prop:loop}, a sign that flips with the budget is not what independent
draws produce. The next two sections show that the closed-loop pattern is an artifact
of how the open-loop arm was replicated, while the semantic prior survives every
replication check.

\section{What the seeds replicate}
\label{sec:replication}
\label{sec:warm}

\paragraph{LLM-Open is one draw per condition.}
LLM-Open generates its pool once per condition and evaluates it on every seed.
Across seeds, the validation-selected member is the \emph{same architecture} in all 12
seeds in five of seven conditions; in the remaining two, two or three distinct members
are ever selected (Table~\ref{tab:matched}, column ``sel.''). The 12 paired seeds
therefore replicate the training data and the angle initialization of essentially one
circuit. They say nothing about how good the next pool from the same model and prompt
would be. This explains the large and erratic open-loop numbers: the $B=4$ pool simply
lacked a strong member (best member mean $0.868$), whereas the $n=6$ and $n=8$ pools each
contained one unusually good circuit.

\paragraph{The score-free prior, replicated per seed.}
LLM-Closed's warm batch is a fresh, score-free call per seed using the same task card.
Its best member (selected by validation) is therefore a per-seed-replicated open-loop
semantic arm with budget $B/2$, and it can be compared with the first $B/2$ draws of
the Random arm on the same seed.
Table~\ref{tab:warm} shows that it beats Random in all seven conditions, by $+0.10$ to
$+0.19$, with 10--11 of 12 seed wins and Holm-adjusted $p\le0.024$ throughout.
The arm of Sec.~\ref{sec:matched}, which uses $B$ score-free proposals from two
independent calls, beats Random by $+0.06$ to $+0.18$ with 9--12 wins.
\emph{The semantic-prior effect is therefore robust when the unit of replication is the
LLM call, not only when it is the seed.}

\section{Feedback with a matched warm start}
\label{sec:matched}

To isolate the closed loop we need an open-loop comparator whose generation is
replicated in the same way. We construct one from logged outputs only.
For seed $s$, \textbf{LLM-Fresh} evaluates the warm batch of seed $s$ followed by the
warm batch generated for seed $s+6 \pmod{12}$; both are score-free calls with an
identical, seed-independent prompt, so the second batch is an exchangeable,
independent draw.
LLM-Closed on seed $s$ evaluates the \emph{same} warm batch followed by its $B/2$
incumbent-conditioned redesigns.
The two arms therefore share their first $B/2$ candidates exactly and differ only in

on fresh score-free proposals. All candidates were re-trained with the same trainer on
the same machine (the re-evaluated LLM-Closed means agree with the committed ones to
within $0.004$); the protocol for this analysis was committed before it ran.

\section{Can a hand-coded prior replace the LLM?}
\label{sec:prior}

The task card states two structural facts: the ground states can be chosen real, and
interactions are nearest-neighbour on the chain. We ask how much of the LLM's
advantage is reproduced by encoding exactly these facts in a sampler, with no language
model. The arms (protocol committed before the runs; Appendix~\ref{app:prior}) sample
the neutral space with the same capacity, trainer and selection, but restrict
rotations to RY (\textbf{RY}), CNOTs to nearest-neighbour pairs (\textbf{NN}), or both
(\textbf{RY+NN}); \textbf{Uniform} is a fresh replicate of Random.
\textbf{Hand} evaluates two fixed templates built from the same facts (RY layers
around a nearest-neighbour CNOT ladder pointing into or out of the latent register;
$B=2$).
\textbf{LLM-shuffled} takes each LLM-Open pool member and randomly permutes its gate
order, preserving its multiset of gates.
We disclose that the restricted arms and the templates were defined after inspecting
the structure of the logged LLM proposals, which use nearest-neighbour CNOTs in
77--100\% and RY in 42--100\% of their gates.

\paragraph{The stated facts are a large part of the prior.}
RY+NN beats Uniform at $n=4,6,8$ (Holm $p\le0.03$), by $+0.034$, $+0.102$ and
$+0.195$: the restriction matters more as the space grows, because the fraction of
nearest-neighbour pairs among all directed pairs is $2/n$. Measured against the
LLM-Open$-$Uniform gap, RY+NN recovers 36\%, 50\% and 58\% of it at $n=4,6,8$ and 88\% on
XXZ. RY alone helps at every width (consistent with Prop.~\ref{prop:real}), whereas NN
alone helps only at $n\ge6$.

\paragraph{The LLM still adds something beyond the two facts.}
LLM-Open beats RY+NN in all three TFIM conditions (12/12 seeds; with the caveat of
Sec.~\ref{sec:replication} that this is one pool per condition), and LLM-Closed, whose
warm batches are replicated per seed, beats it at $n=4$ (12/12, Holm $p=0.006$) and
with 9--10 of 12 wins elsewhere, without surviving Holm correction at $n=6,8$ or on XXZ.

\paragraph{Gate order carries much of the remainder.}
Randomly permuting the gates of each LLM-Open pool member costs $0.038$, $0.093$ and
$0.156$ at $n=4,6,8$ (9--12 of 12 seeds), which brings LLM-shuffled down to the level of
RY+NN at $n=6,8$. On XXZ the permuted circuits are slightly \emph{better}. At larger
widths, then, what the LLM contributes beyond gate statistics is mostly the ordering
of a nearest-neighbour ladder---exactly what the hand templates hard-code.

\paragraph{Two hand templates nearly close the gap.}
The two templates, evaluated with $B=2$, reach $0.942$, $0.900$, $0.854$ and $0.948$
(TFIM $n=4,6,8$; XXZ). LLM-Open exceeds them by only $0.018$--$0.023$ on TFIM and is
\emph{below} them on XXZ ($-0.026$, 0/12). LLM-Closed is not distinguishable from them
at $n=6,8$ and exceeds them by $0.009$--$0.025$ at $n=4$. Expressed as a fraction of the
LLM-Open$-$Uniform gap, the templates recover 80\%, 89\% and 93\% at $n=4,6,8$.

\paragraph{The prior in units of evaluations.}
Prop.~\ref{prop:budget} turns tail masses into equivalent budgets.
At $n=4$, 21\% of score-free LLM proposals (warm batches, $N=48$) reach a test fidelity
of $0.95$, against $0.26\%$ of uniform draws ($N=768$) and $2.1\%$ of RY+NN draws, so
eight LLM proposals match roughly 700 uniform or 90 RY+NN draws at this level.
At $n=6$ the level $0.80$ is reached by 33\% of LLM proposals, $0.26\%$ of uniform and
18\% of RY+NN draws ($B_{\rm eq}\approx1{,}200$ and $\approx16$); at $n=8$, 10\% of LLM
proposals reach $0.80$ while no uniform or RY+NN draw does in 768 and 384 trials, so
$B_{\rm eq}>220$ uniform draws (95\% bound).
These equivalences idealize selection as noiseless and should be read as orders of
magnitude. They also locate the LLM's value: large against an uninformed sampler, an
order of magnitude smaller against a sampler that encodes the prompt's two sentences,
and smaller still against a two-circuit template.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Where the LLM's value lies on this task.}
Every check that replicates LLM generation per seed finds a large, consistent benefit
of score-free LLM proposals over uninformed search (Table~\ref{tab:warm}), and none
finds a benefit of the closed feedback loop once the open-loop comparator is
replicated the same way (Table~\ref{tab:matched}). In the language of
Prop.~\ref{prop:loop}, incumbent-conditioned redesigns were not detectably more likely
to produce a better circuit than fresh score-free proposals from the same model.
Our results thus support the view, also reached for quantum reservoir design with a
different protocol \cite{bhatia2026qrc}, that on small-budget circuit design the useful
part of an LLM is a proposal distribution, not an autonomous optimizer.

\paragraph{How much of that prior is ``reasoning''?}
On this task, much of the prior can be written down in two sentences and two
templates. The task card's statements that amplitudes are real and interactions
nearest-neighbour, turned into a sampler, recover 36--88\% of the LLM's advantage over
uniform search, and two fixed ladder templates recover 80--93\%. Whatever the LLM adds on
top is concentrated in gate ordering. We therefore do not interpret the results as
evidence that the model discovers unstated physics: the benchmark measures whether a
model can \emph{exploit} structure that the prompt states, and an expert can exploit the
same structure with a template. The value of the LLM in this setting is that it does
this translation without a human writing the template, across tasks whose structure
is described only in text---a claim that a benchmark with less obvious structure
would test more sharply.

\paragraph{Methodological lessons.}
(i) An open-loop LLM arm that reuses one generated pool across seeds has an effective
sample size of one for generation; seed-level $p$-values for it can be very small and
still say nothing about the next pool. In our data this produced a spurious
closed-loop advantage at one operating point (reference cell, $p=0.012$), a spurious
large advantage at $B=4$, and spurious significant reversals at $n=6,8$.
(ii) Hand-coded priors derived from the prompt are a cheap and necessary control: in
three of four tasks they closed most of the gap to the LLM.
(iii) Knowing the compression ceiling (Prop.~\ref{prop:ceiling}) prevents
misreading shortfalls as data limits; here every shortfall is a search or training
limit.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

All simulations are noiseless state-vector simulations with at most eight qubits, one
capacity rule ($3n$ rotations, $n$ CNOTs), one short training schedule, two Hamiltonian
families and two model snapshots. Twelve paired seeds detect large effects and miss
small ones: the matched-warm ablation excludes closed-loop effects larger than about
$0.07$ at $n=6,8$ but not smaller ones.
The one-factor design estimates no interactions (e.g., large $B$ at large $n$).
The matched-warm and per-seed analyses reuse logged LLM outputs; LLM-Fresh pairs batch
$s$ with batch $s+6$, so each warm batch appears in two seeds' Fresh arms.
The prior-matched arms and templates were specified after inspecting the LLM
proposals (Sec.~\ref{sec:prior}); a fully blind test would fix such baselines before
any LLM output is seen.
The test grid is shared across seeds, conditions and the five development stages
(Appendix~\ref{app:history}); conclusions rest on contrasts that do not depend on the
development choices, but a fresh grid would be cleaner.
Invalid LLM proposals are replaced by random draws inside the score, so architecture
quality and contract compliance are entangled, most strongly for the alternative model.
We ran no prompt ablation that removes the physics sentences; the prior-matched arms
show what those sentences are worth to a sampler, not to the model.
No claim of quantum advantage, hardware relevance or general LLM superiority is made.

\section{Conclusion}

Under exact capacity and budget control, LLM proposals improve variational
quantum-autoencoder architecture search through their score-free prior, consistently
across budgets, widths, Hamiltonian families and two model snapshots, when the unit of
replication is the LLM call. The closed feedback loop adds no detectable value once the
open-loop comparator is replicated in the same way; the apparent closed-loop advantages
and reversals in a conventional analysis came from evaluating one open-loop pool on
every seed. Most of the prior is reproduced by encoding the prompt's two structural
facts in a sampler, and nearly all of it by two hand-written templates; what remains is
largely gate ordering. We recommend that evaluations of LLM circuit designers replicate
generation, report hand-coded priors built from the prompt, and state the task's
information-theoretic ceiling.

\section{Exact LLM prompt}
\label{app:prompt}

\paragraph{System instruction.}
\begin{quote}
You are a quantum-autoencoder circuit architect. Your job is architecture proposal, not numerical optimization. Respect the exact resource budget. Use the physical structure in the task description to propose circuit hypotheses. Return circuit structure only.
\end{quote}

\paragraph{Task instruction (v3/v4).}
\begin{quote}
Compress ground states of the 4-qubit open-chain transverse-field Ising Hamiltonian
$H=-\sum Z_iZ_{i+1}-h\sum X_i$, $h\in[0.2,2.0]$, into latent qubits $q_0,q_1$ while trash qubits $q_2,q_3$ should end in $\lvert00\rangle$.
The Hamiltonian is real in the computational basis, so its ground-state amplitudes can be chosen real.
Interactions are nearest-neighbor on the open chain $q_0$--$q_1$--$q_2$--$q_3$, so nearest-neighbor correlations are important.
Resource contract (exact, non-negotiable): the encoder is an ordered sequence of exactly 16 gates --- exactly 12 single-qubit rotations, each RX, RY, or RZ on any qubit (no per-axis quota), and exactly 4 CNOTs, each with any control and any different target (the same pair may repeat at different positions).
You choose the gate ORDER freely.
You do not choose rotation angles: a shared numerical optimizer trains all 12 angles identically for every method.
[Open loop only:] Output 8 distinct candidates.
Prefer hypotheses that (1) preserve a real-valued representation when useful and (2) route correlations/information from the trash qubits toward the latent qubits.
\end{quote}

\paragraph{JSON schema card (v3).}
\begin{quote}\small
Return ONLY a single JSON object, no prose and no markdown fences, with this schema:\\
\texttt{\{"candidates": [\{"name": "<short-id>", "gates": [}\\
\texttt{\ \ \{"g": "RY", "q": 0\}, \{"g": "CNOT", "c": 3, "t": 2\}, ...]\}]\}}\\
Each gates list holds exactly 16 entries in execution order: a rotation is \texttt{\{"g": "RX"|"RY"|"RZ", "q": 0-3\}} and a CNOT is \texttt{\{"g": "CNOT", "c": 0-3, "t": 0-3, c != t\}}. Exactly 12 rotations and exactly 4 CNOTs per candidate.
\end{quote}
If returned candidates violate the capacity contract, up to two bounded repair calls quote the rejected entries and errors and request replacements (pre-declared amendment); only then are remaining slots filled by flagged random draws.
The v4 LLM-Closed warm start uses the same task instruction with ``Output 4 distinct candidates. Aim for structural DIVERSITY across the candidates (different orderings, wirings, and axis patterns), \ldots'' in place of the open-loop closing lines.
The v1/v2 pilot used the same physics sentences with a rigid layered layout and a per-layer axes schema; that version is preserved in the repository history and \texttt{docs/research/LLM\_QAE\_SKILL.md}.

\paragraph{v5 incumbent-redesign prompt (primary closed loop).}
Each v5 refinement call sends, verbatim (with the task card above and the JSON schema card below):
\begin{quote}\small
You are improving the currently best quantum-autoencoder architecture. [\emph{task card}]\\
Current best architecture (execution order): \texttt{<full 16-gate list>}\\
Current validation trash fidelity: \texttt{<F\_val>}\\
You may redesign the architecture freely. Your next proposal may modify one gate choice, several coordinated choices, or the entire architecture, provided the exact resource budget is preserved: exactly 12 single-qubit rotations (each RX, RY, or RZ), exactly 4 CNOTs, exactly 16 gates total, four qubits, arbitrary valid ordering, arbitrary valid CNOT control/target. You may retain the incumbent conceptually, modify it locally, or redesign it substantially. Do not propose numerical rotation angles --- a shared optimizer trains all 12 angles. Do not repeat the incumbent or any previously evaluated architecture exactly.\\
Use the physical structure of the task --- the real-valued ground states, nearest-neighbor Ising interactions, and latent/trash roles --- to propose an architecture you expect to outperform the incumbent. Return one complete new architecture.
\end{quote}
The call also requests logged-only metadata: \texttt{strategy} (local\_adjustment / topology\_redesign / rotation\_redesign / global\_redesign), a one-sentence \texttt{rationale}, and \texttt{preserved}/\texttt{changed} phrases.

\paragraph{v3/v4 history-conditioned feedback template (superseded).}
Earlier closed-loop versions appended, per evaluated candidate:
\begin{quote}\small
candidate \texttt{<canonical JSON>} validation trash fidelity: \texttt{<value>} duplicate: \texttt{<true/false>}\\

\section{Development history of the reference cell}
\label{app:history}

The reference cell was reached through a sequence of designs on the same task and the
same 64-point test grid. We report them because the closed-loop interface was revised
twice after seeing earlier results; the reference-cell closed-loop advantage is the
outcome of the third interface tried and is therefore treated as
hypothesis-generating (Sec.~\ref{sec:design}).

\paragraph{Earlier syntactic benchmarks.}
Before the QAE task, capacity-controlled benchmarks on a synthetic regression task
(T1) compared Random, Evolutionary and Greedy search (no LLM arm) under matched
capacity, trainer and budget $B=25$; median protected-test RMSE was $0.01775$,
$0.01715$ and $0.01689$, and all pairs were practically equivalent within a
pre-specified margin $\delta=0.002$, while best-of-$B$ selection beat a fixed circuit.
An earlier, uncontrolled LLM advantage in the project disappeared once circuit size was
matched. These observations motivated a task in which the proposer is given structure
to reason about; they carry no information about LLM performance on T1 itself.

\paragraph{v1/v2.} A rigid rotation-layer/CNOT-layer layout. The LLM pool beat an
RY-only random control, an equal-budget evolutionary search and a hand-designed encoder
(mean $0.9700$ against $0.9424$, $0.9005$, $0.8889$). Because the layout differs from
the neutral space, these numbers are not comparable with the main text.

\paragraph{v3 (neutral space, single-start adaptive arms).} LLM-Open $0.9653$,
LLM-Closed $0.9553$ (all eight proposals sequential, conditioned on the full history),
Random $0.8721$, Greedy $0.7667$. Greedy fell below Random ($-0.1055$, $p=0.005$)
because one random start left too little exploration at $B=8$. LLM-Closed $-$ LLM-Open
was $-0.0100$ ($p=0.68$); 26 of 96 closed-loop proposals were duplicates or invalid.

\paragraph{v4 (4+4 allocation, history-conditioned refinement).} Greedy recovered to
parity with Random ($0.8793$ vs.\ $0.8677$, $p=0.42$); LLM-Open reached $0.9643$; LLM-Closed
$-$ LLM-Open was $-0.0085$ ($p=0.13$); 8 of 48 refinement slots fell back to random draws.

\paragraph{v5 = reference cell (4+4, incumbent-conditioned free redesign).}
LLM-Closed $-$ LLM-Open $=+0.0066$ ($[+0.0023,+0.0112]$, $d_z=0.81$, 11/12,
$p=0.012$). Refinement gains over the best warm start were similar for Greedy
($+0.0159$) and LLM-Closed ($+0.0143$; 6 improved, 6 unchanged; zeros excluded from the
Wilcoxon test). Of 48 closed-loop refinement slots, 40 were genuine proposals and 8
were flagged fallbacks (all for wrong gate counts); there were no duplicates.
7 of the 40 proposals were accepted. Proposals changed a median of 11 of 16 positions,
but position-wise counts overstate edit size (one insertion shifts all later
positions), and acceptance was not higher for large changes (1/4 for proposals
within four positions, 6/36 otherwise), so we do not interpret the changes as evidence
of ``coordinated global redesign''.
Applying a Bonferroni factor of three for the three closed-loop interfaces tried on
this test grid gives $p=0.037$ for the v5 contrast.

\section{Prior-matched baselines: protocol summary}
\label{app:prior}

The protocol (file \path{docs/research/QAE_PRIOR_BASELINES_PROTOCOL.md} in the repository) and its
Addendum~A (matched-warm ablation) were committed and pushed before any of their
candidates was trained. Pools per seed: Uniform 64 draws; RY, NN, RY+NN 32 draws
each; LLM-shuffled 4 random permutations of each of the 8 LLM-Open pool members; Hand
the two templates below. The $B$-budget arm of a pool is its first $B$ draws.
Random streams are seeded by (study tag, arm, $n$, family, seed).
With $h=n/2$, \texttt{ladder-in} is: RY on every qubit; CNOT $q_{i+1}\to q_i$ for
$i=n-2,\dots,0$; RY on every qubit; CNOT $q_h\to q_{h-1}$; RY on every qubit.
\texttt{ladder-out} reverses every CNOT.
Training uses a tensor-contraction implementation of the shared trainer that agrees
with the original dense implementation to $10^{-15}$ on every checked candidate.
In total the study trained 9{,}312 prior-matched and 1{,}080 matched-warm candidates and
made no LLM call. Figure~\ref{fig:budget} averages 2{,}000 random $B$-subsets per seed.
